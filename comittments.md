# Group Contract

## Introduction

Here is a template for the group contract.

You can choose whether you want to work according to agile methods, but it is strongly recommended that you continue practicing these skills. It also provides structure for working with backlog refinement, planning poker, sprint planning, and retrospectives.

You are free to add/remove sections, but there is an extremely clear connection between well-written group contracts and well-executed work/portfolio cases.

## Contract Contents

### Previous Group Work Experiences

- What are each group member's top 3 worst experiences with group work? What "negative expectations" are you bringing into this?

- No-shows.
- Tight deadlines, people not informing you about how much work they have done or don't have time, etc. It is good to communicate in time so that the team notices before deadlines and can help finish these tasks in time before the deadline.
- If you have a hard time finishing a task, ask for help or guidance instead of doing nothing and stressing about it. People in the group will be more than willing to help or support you in finishing tasks.

> Example: People don't do what they have promised within the agreed time and say that "they are working on it", but it never gets finished.

> **TIP**
>
> It is good to get the negative expectations out in the open and discuss previous experiences openly. The more open you are in your communication now, the better group work you will have. At a workplace, you may already know each other and there may be other "requirements". For some reason, many people behave differently in a school environment when it comes to group work 🤔

- Time for backlog refinement every week (approximately 1 hour).

**Monday/Wednesday**

- Time for sprint planning every week (approximately 1–2 hours).

**Monday**

**Mondays/Fridays**

### Personality Type

> It may feel uncomfortable to discuss the following, but a foundation for good collaboration is having some self-awareness as well as understanding how your group members function in different situations.

How does each group member react under stress?

- Runs away from the situation, stops participating, ignores the situation/group work
- Becomes confrontational/aggressive
- Freezes up, doesn't know what to do, has difficulty getting started and/or moving forward
- Tries to make everyone feel good/get along
- In some other way?

What role does each group member usually take in group work? What role do you take "quietly" for yourself?

- **Leader:** Driven, dynamic, takes initiative.
- **Planner/Implementer:** Organized, practical, reliable.
- **Expert/Specialist:** Deep knowledge, focused, analytical.
- **Information Seeker:** Curious, fact-based, gathers data.
- **Completer/Quality Assurer:** Thorough, detail-oriented, finishes on time.
- **Innovator/Idea Generator:** Creative, imaginative, sees new solutions.
- **Reviewer/Evaluator:** Critical, strategic, objective, logical.
- **Devil's Advocate:** Challenging, questions things constructively.
- **Team Player/Coordinator:** Cooperative, diplomatic, supportive.
- **Motivator:** Positive, empathetic, creates a good atmosphere.
- **Gatekeeper/Process Facilitator:** Ensures participation, manages the discussion.

### Scrum Master

It is recommended that you appoint a Scrum Master who is responsible for *delegating* tasks. The Scrum Master should not *do everything*, but make sure everyone has something to work on and that nobody gets stuck.

If you want to rotate the role, do so, but it is also perfectly fine if one person is comfortable taking on the role.

If nobody wants to be Scrum Master, you should agree on who will steer the ship in another way.

## Working in a Group

Working in a group is hardly easy.

Here are some tips:

- Break tasks down so that they take approximately half a day to complete.
- Write pseudocode for the tasks/explain clearly what they involve, preferably as a group.
- Don't be afraid to ask for help. Don't be afraid to say that you don't know something.
- Direct and open communication is best: if something isn't working, bring it up with the group, refer to your commitments, and ask questions!
- Silence, absence, and lack of communication are guaranteed triggers for group problems 💥🤫

> **TIP**
>
> **"I'm the only one working in the group!" 😠**

1. Check the repository's contribution graphs and verify that this is actually the case.
2. Ask your group members whether they are stuck on something and whether you should look at something together to help them move forward.
3. If the response is instead excuses such as "I haven't had time because of work/illness/I had other priorities/I had to do XYZ" — OK. Shit happens. That's how it will be in real life too.
4. Have a group meeting, go through your group contract, and go through point 5 below.
5. During the group meeting, break the project down together into X number of tasks so that everyone has an equal number of tasks. Decide who does what, and get on with your part.
6. Then continue working on your tasks.

Everyone is an adult attending this education program, and at some point personal commitment and personal responsibility are also required. A vocational higher education program should be fairly close to working life, and if the group and group dynamics aren't working, you would normally go to your immediate manager and bring up the problem constructively, along with some suggestions you have thought through.

If you also choose not to contribute to the project, you miss out on valuable skills such as:

- Working in a team (which you have to do in working life)
- Working in a team on code (it will be brutal if you haven't practiced it at school)
- Communicating about code (this will also be brutal if you don't practice it)
- Communication and how to create a functioning group; everyone is responsible for contributing
- By explaining things, you learn an incredible amount and become a much better teammate

> **TIP**
>
> **"We have a difficult group member."**
>
> See the section **"I'm the only one working in the group."**

> **TIP**
>
> **"The others are so much better than I am."**
>
> Unfortunately, that's also how it is in working life. In this case, you need to ask questions, ask for explanations, and become comfortable with not knowing.
>
> The better you communicate and the more ambitiously you study, the better the end result will be.
>
> If you sit alone in silence, frustration in the group is **guaranteed** to grow.

> **TIP**
>
> **"The others are much worse than I am."**
>
> Unfortunately, that's also how it is in working life. Surrounded by idiots.
>
> But not everyone has the same prior knowledge, circumstances, or "logical ability" out of the box. It takes some time to switch over to programming thinking.
>
> See it as an opportunity for you to learn how to communicate about code. The better you can talk about and explain code, the more appreciated you will become as a team member, and the more often you will end up in (fun and advanced) projects where good collaboration and communication are required for a good end result. Fact.
